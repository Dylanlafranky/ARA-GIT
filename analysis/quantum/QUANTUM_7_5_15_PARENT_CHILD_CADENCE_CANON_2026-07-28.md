## Bidirectional asymmetry inheritance and constraint

Coarse-graining does not erase child asymmetry. A child's asymmetry feeds the
corresponding asymmetry of the parent above. The parent state also acts back
downward through its coupling to the child, biasing, sustaining, constraining
or disrupting its next expressions. The `15` parent must also contain or
couple to a still-to-be-identified complementary parent-level contribution.
After applying any required rung-orientation flip, let

\[
x_{C,k}=1+\delta_C,
\qquad
x_{Y,k}=1+\delta_Y,
\]

where \(C\) is the complete observed `7.5` child identity and \(Y\) is the
unknown complement. For the simplest equal-weight parent projection,

\[

=
\frac{x_{C,k}+x_{Y,k}}{2}
=
1+\frac{\delta_C+\delta_Y}{2}.
\]

Therefore:

- same-direction coupled asymmetries reinforce and move the parent to the same
  side of its ridge;
- unequal opposing asymmetries leave their signed difference in the parent;
- only equal, oppositely oriented contributions cancel to a parent
  `1.0`; and
- a parent `1.0` can hide active asymmetric contributors when their signed
  contributions cancel at that measurement grain.

For unequal participation, external coupling or an unresolved channel, use
declared weights and `Other`:

\[

=
w_C\delta_C+w_Y\delta_Y+\delta_{\rm Other},
\qquad
w_C+w_Y=1.
\]

Plainly: the child's asymmetry is inherited by the adult. What can change
under the rung shift is whether that asymmetry remains visibly perpendicular
as `Other`, becomes part of the parent's main diameter, is modified by an
orientation flip, or is cancelled by an equal counter-asymmetry.
