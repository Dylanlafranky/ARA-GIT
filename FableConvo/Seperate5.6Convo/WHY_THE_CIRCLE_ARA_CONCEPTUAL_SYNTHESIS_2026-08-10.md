## Abstract

Why does ARA keep returning to the circle?

The answer is not simply that circles are common in nature, nor that oscillations are often drawn as waves. The stronger reason is geometric. A circle is the simplest smooth closed planar form in which movement can continue without a privileged seam, relational turning can be distributed continuously rather than concentrated at discrete corners, and scale can change without changing the identity of the geometry. Locally, a sufficiently large circle is indistinguishable from a line; globally, it remains closed. Under rotation and projection, the same circular or spherical identity can appear as a circle, ellipse, line-like cut, or other reduced coordinate. In phase descriptions, a complete oscillation naturally closes around a circle. Decompressed across orientation, the circle becomes the sphere.

These properties match the central requirements ARA places on a pure identity: closure, continuous movement, retained relation, scale-free renormalisation, orientation-dependent measurement, and recursion into larger identities without erasing smaller ones.

This paper separates three layers throughout:

1. **Established geometry and physics:** what follows from ordinary mathematics or standard physical representations.
2. **ARA crosswalk:** how those established structures map onto ARA terminology.
3. **ARA hypothesis:** the stronger claim that recursively nested sphere/wave closure is not merely a useful coordinate system but an underlying relational geometry of physical identity.

The central proposal is therefore not that every visible object is literally a Euclidean circle. It is that **pure relational identity in ARA is modelled as continuous closure, while observed departures from that closure encode projection, interaction, history, scale, and coupling.**

# 1. The question is not “where do circles appear?”

The weak version of the argument would be easy:

- planets are round,
- waves are periodic,
- phase diagrams contain circles,
- eyes are spherical,
- orbits can be approximately circular,
- bubbles and droplets become round under certain conditions.

None of those facts is enough to establish the circle as a primitive geometry.

ARA asks a more demanding question:

> **What geometry can remain the same object while continuously moving, changing scale, changing measurement orientation, becoming locally line-like, and being embedded as a complete child inside a larger complete parent?**

The circle becomes interesting because it satisfies this collection of requirements unusually cleanly.

The circle is therefore not chosen because it is visually common. It is chosen because it is a natural candidate for **continuous relational closure**.

A compact statement is:

> **A circle is closure with no privileged handover point.**

The sphere is the corresponding directionally decompressed identity:

> **A sphere is closure with no privileged orientation.**

ARA's stronger hypothesis is that these are not merely convenient representations. They are the undistorted geometry toward which an identity resolves when interaction, projection, and cross-rung coupling are separated from the identity being measured.

That final step is a hypothesis. The geometry leading up to it is not.

# 2. From corners and edges to continuous relation

A finite polygon makes relational structure visually explicit.

Take a triangle. It has three vertices and three edges. One can read the vertices as identity locations and the edges as relations between them:

\[
A \xrightarrow{R_{AB}} B
\xrightarrow{R_{BC}} C
\xrightarrow{R_{CA}} A.
\]

The same idea extends to a regular \(n\)-gon:

\[
p_1 \rightarrow p_2 \rightarrow \cdots \rightarrow p_n \rightarrow p_1.
\]

Closure is achieved by a finite sequence of identity locations and relational handovers.

This is a useful teaching picture for Information³: identities alone do not specify the whole object; the relations between them are load-bearing.

But the circle does something different.

For a regular \(n\)-gon, the exterior turn at each vertex is

\[
\Delta\theta = \frac{2\pi}{n}.
\]

There are \(n\) such turns, so the total turning around the closed object is always

\[
n\Delta\theta = 2\pi.
\]

As

\[
n\rightarrow\infty,
\]

the turn at each individual vertex tends to zero:

\[
\Delta\theta\rightarrow0,
\]

while total closure remains

\[
2\pi.
\]

Mathematically, a circle does **not** have infinitely many literal corners. It has no vertices at all. The more useful description is:

> **The discrete turning of the polygon has become continuously distributed around the whole boundary.**

In ARA language, a finite polygon concentrates handover into named junctions. The circle distributes handover everywhere.

There is no special edge on which relation “lives.”  
There is no special corner at which movement “changes direction.”  
The whole boundary continuously carries both.

That is why the circle is more interesting than “a polygon with infinitely many sides.” The discrete distinction between identity-node and relation-edge has become a continuously resolved relation.

A teaching compression is:

> Triangle: “I turn in three places.”  
> Square: “I turn in four.”  
> Pentagon: “I turn in five.”  
> Circle: **“I am turning everywhere.”**

# 3. The circle is movement without loss of closure

A circle can be parameterised by phase:

\[
x(\theta)=R\cos\theta,
\qquad
y(\theta)=R\sin\theta.
\]

As \(\theta\) changes, the state changes continuously. The point moves. Its direction changes continuously. Yet the identity of the closure remains unchanged:

\[
x^2+y^2=R^2.
\]

This gives ARA a clean geometric model of a distinction that is easy to flatten:

- **Connection does not mean stasis.**
- **Movement does not require loss of identity.**

A circular identity can be completely dynamic while retaining its defining relation.

This is close to the current ARA reading of a dynamic lock:

\[
\text{retained relation}
+
\text{ongoing traversal}
\rightarrow
\text{persistent identity}.
\]

The circle therefore contains both of the tendencies that ARA often describes separately when looking at a projection:

- Connection: the relation remains closed and recoverable.
- Movement: the phase continues to advance.

The circle is not a frozen wave. It is what a complete traversal looks like when its phase closes.

# 4. Circle and wave are two readings of one traversal

Standard mathematics already contains an exact bridge between oscillation and circular phase.

Using complex phase,

\[
e^{i\theta}
=
\cos\theta+i\sin\theta.
\]

A complete phase traversal

\[
0\rightarrow2\pi
\]

returns to the starting phase while having passed continuously through every intermediate phase.

Likewise, a harmonic oscillator can be rescaled into phase-space variables satisfying

\[
Q^2+P^2=2H,
\]

which is a circle at fixed energy \(H\).

This does **not** mean every physical wave is a literal spatial circle. It means that a complete oscillatory state often admits an exact circular representation in phase space.

ARA makes a stronger identification:

> **A wave is the traversal reading of closure; a circle is the closed reading of that traversal.**

Under this language, “wave” and “circle” are not rival shapes. They are two projections of the same relational cycle.

The public scientific fence is important:

- Harmonic phase-space circles are established mathematics.
- Complex phase rotations are established mathematics.
- “All pure physical identities are ultimately closed waves/spheres” is an ARA hypothesis.

# 5. Local straightness does not imply global straightness

The next important property is curvature.

For a circle of radius \(R\),

\[
\kappa=\frac{1}{R}.
\]

As \(R\) becomes very large,

\[
R\rightarrow\infty
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\kappa\rightarrow0.
\]

A sufficiently small arc of a sufficiently large circle becomes arbitrarily well approximated by a straight line.

So:

> **A line can be the local appearance of curvature whose closure lies outside the current observation window.**

In ordinary Euclidean geometry, a finite straight line is not literally a hidden finite-radius circle. But the limiting relationship is exact: a circle approaches a line as its radius tends to infinity.

There is an even closer mathematical analogue in inversive geometry, where circles and straight lines are treated together as **generalised circles**. A line can be represented as a circle passing through the point at infinity.

That does not prove that every physical line closes somewhere in the universe.

It does establish a powerful crosswalk:

> **“Line” and “circle” need not be fundamentally unrelated geometric categories. Their difference can depend on scale, chart, or projection.**

This matters because ARA proposes:

\[
\text{straight at rung }k
\approx
\text{small arc of closure at rung }k+1.
\]

The stronger ARA hypothesis is that this recursion continues: what looks globally straight at one resolved boundary may itself be locally straight only because the parent curvature is too shallow to resolve.

That is not established physics. But it is geometrically coherent.

# 6. The full extrusion: a local circle carried by a larger vector

This is the part that is easy to under-read.

Consider a local closed motion \(c_k(t)\) carried by a larger-scale trajectory \(X_{k+1}(t)\):

\[
r(t)=X_{k+1}(t)+c_k(t).
\]

If the parent trajectory appears straight over the current window, the local circle may extrude into a helix-like or translated trajectory.

But ARA does not stop there.

The proposal is that the apparently straight parent vector may itself be an unresolved arc of a much larger closure:

\[
X_{k+1}(t)
\subset
\Omega_{k+1}.
\]

Then:

\[
\text{local closure}_k
+
\text{parent carriage}_{k+1}
\rightarrow
\text{larger closure}_{k+1}.
\]

And the same move can occur again:

\[
\text{closure}_k
+
\text{carriage}_{k+1}
\rightarrow
\text{closure}_{k+1}
+
\text{carriage}_{k+2}
\rightarrow\cdots
\]

This is the geometry behind the intuitive statement:

> **The circle can appear to extrude away from us indefinitely, while the extrusion itself is only a local arc of a larger circle outside our connected perception.**

A familiar kinematic analogy is Earth.

A point on Earth can return to the same local rotational phase after one day while the Earth itself has moved along its orbit. “Same local phase” is not “same total state.”

The local cycle closes:

\[
\theta_k(t+T_k)=\theta_k(t),
\]

while the parent state has changed:

\[
X_{k+1}(t+T_k)\neq X_{k+1}(t).
\]

This gives a precise mathematical skeleton for the ARA phrase:

> **The circle comes home, but home has moved.**

If the parent carriage also closes, then the child closure has become part of a larger closed identity.

# 7. Why the circle can scale indefinitely

The circle has no preferred radius.

If

\[
x^2+y^2=R^2,
\]

and every length is scaled by \(\lambda>0\),

\[
x\rightarrow\lambda x,
\qquad
y\rightarrow\lambda y,
\qquad
R\rightarrow\lambda R,
\]

then

\[
(\lambda x)^2+(\lambda y)^2=(\lambda R)^2.
\]

The magnitude changes. The relational form does not.

This is ordinary similarity geometry.

It gives the circle an essential property for recursion:

> **The same closure law works at every size.**

Nothing in circular geometry itself specifies a smallest circle or largest circle.

That does **not** establish that physical reality contains infinitely many rungs in both directions. It means only that the geometry itself imposes no scale-dependent stopping rule.

This is where ARA adds a separate hypothesis: a recursive ladder of relational identities.

Importantly:

> **The circle does not force the octave.**

A circle can be scaled by any positive \(\lambda\). The specific rule

\[
R_{k+1}=2R_k
\]

is additional ARA structure, not a theorem about circles.

The circle answers:

> Can the same geometry survive arbitrary rescaling?

Yes.

The octave asks:

> Which scale relation is selected between particular recursive rungs?

That is a separate empirical and theoretical question.

# 8. TE-ARA: complete locally, different in native magnitude

This scale invariance is also why TE-ARA must not be confused with native size.

A small identity can be locally complete:

\[
TE_k=2,
\]

while a much larger parent is also locally complete:

\[
TE_{k+1}=2.
\]

The smaller identity is not “less complete” because its native radius, duration, energy, or bandwidth is smaller.

ARA therefore separates:

\[
\text{local completeness}
\]

from

\[
\text{native magnitude}.
\]

A useful conceptual notation is:

\[
\Omega_k\subset\Omega_{k+1},
\]

where the containment is relational rather than a claim that every child must literally sit spatially inside the parent like nested Russian dolls.

The child closure remains part of the parent's internal history and structure. Promotion to the parent does not erase the child.

This becomes especially important when comparing identities at very different rungs.

If the ARA scale ladder is octave-based, one can define a rung coordinate

\[
n(R)=\log_2\left(\frac{R}{R_0}\right).
\]

Then the separation between identity \(A\) and parent \(P\) is

\[
\Delta n_{A\rightarrow P}
=
\log_2\left(\frac{R_P}{R_A}\right).
\]

Two children can each be locally complete while having very different rung separations from the same parent.

This is the geometric basis of the recent fruit-fly / human / Earth thought experiment.

The fly and the human can each be assigned full local participation at their own rung:

\[
TE_F=TE_H=2,
\]

while

\[
\Delta n_{F\rightarrow E}
\neq
\Delta n_{H\rightarrow E}.
\]

The hypothesis being explored is not that a human second equals a fly second. It is that **the same parent cycle may occupy a different relational depth when resolved from children separated from the parent by different numbers of rungs.**

Whether subjective temporal experience actually follows that geometry is an open ARA hypothesis, not established psychophysics.

# 9. The sphere is not a different ontology

The circle is the planar closure.

ARA's full identity is better represented by the sphere.

A sphere of radius \(R\) is

\[
x^2+y^2+z^2=R^2.
\]

A plane through its centre cuts a great circle.

A diameter through it gives a one-dimensional oriented cut.

This naturally matches the current ARA interpretation of the \(0\!-\!2\) scalar:

\[
x_n=P_n(\Omega)\in[0,2].
\]

The scalar is not the whole sphere. It is one oriented diameter through it.

Rotate the diameter and the measurement changes while the identity can remain the same.

Thus:

\[
\text{different cut}
\neq
\text{different identity}.
\]

Decompression means recovering more cuts until the larger closure becomes visible.

This is one reason the sphere is more appropriate than treating ARA as a one-dimensional number line. The number line is a measurement coordinate. The sphere is the proposed identity being measured.

The circle and sphere are therefore not separate primitives:

- circle = a closed planar traversal / cut;
- sphere = the directionally decompressed whole;
- wave = the same closure read through traversal;
- \(0\!-\!2\) = one oriented diameter through the identity.

# 10. Projection can change appearance without changing identity

A circle embedded in three dimensions does not always look circular in projection.

Viewed face-on, it appears circular.

Tilted, an orthographic projection appears elliptical.

Viewed edge-on, it can collapse toward a line segment.

So one identity can present as:

\[
\text{circle}
\rightarrow
\text{ellipse}
\rightarrow
\text{line-like projection}
\]

as observation orientation changes.

This is an exact geometric example of:

> **Projection is not identity.**

ARA extends the same logic beyond visual projection.

Let \(W\) be an underlying relational object and \(P_n\) a measurement along direction or channel \(n\):

\[
m_n=P_n(W).
\]

For a simple vector projection,

\[
m_n=W\cdot n.
\]

A large underlying object can produce almost no signal in a nearly orthogonal measurement direction:

\[
W\cdot n\approx0.
\]

So:

\[
\text{little or nothing in one cut}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{little or nothing in the identity}.
\]

This supplies a mathematical language for **connected perception**.

Humans directly access only some coupling channels. Other identities or properties become measurable only after an additional connection chain is built:

\[
\text{unseen process}
\rightarrow
\text{coupling}
\rightarrow
\text{detector}
\rightarrow
\text{human-readable signal}.
\]

Electromagnetic radiation outside human vision is an ordinary example. It existed before humans developed instruments that translated it into channels we could access.

Dark matter is a more extreme scientific analogy: whatever its ultimate nature, its existence is inferred through gravitational effects despite the absence of ordinary direct electromagnetic visibility.

ARA's stronger proposal is that entire parent closures may be weakly or orthogonally coupled to a child measurement. Their absence from one cut would then be a statement about the relation, not necessarily the identity.

# 11. The eye as a teaching model

The eye provides a small-scale geometric demonstration of several of these ideas at once.

The eyeball is approximately spherical and rotates within a constrained socket. When a person “looks left” or “looks right,” ordinary language encourages a line-like description:

\[
\text{left}\longrightarrow\text{right}.
\]

But the physical action is rotational. A sphere changes orientation and the gaze ray sweeps through angle.

So an apparently linear behavioural coordinate can be generated by a rotating spherical identity.

The eye is also sampled through different behavioural modes: fixation, rapid saccadic rotation, smooth pursuit, blinking, and head/body motion. The parent visual percept can remain stable even while its child operations are intermittent, rotational, or highly non-uniform.

The teaching point is not that the eye proves ARA.

It is that a familiar human system already demonstrates:

\[
\text{curved/rotational child dynamics}
\rightarrow
\text{line-like coordinate}
\]

and

\[
\text{interrupted or jerky child operations}
\rightarrow
\text{smooth parent percept}.
\]

The smoothness assigned to one rung need not be the smoothness seen from another.

# 12. Time as relational slices

Time is a useful conceptual example because humans do not directly hold “time itself” as an object.

Operationally, we compare change against change.

A clock supplies an ordered sequence of reference states. Another system supplies an ordered sequence of target states. Elapsed time is reconstructed through their relation.

Schematically:

\[
\Delta(\text{target state})
\quad\text{relative to}\quad
\Delta(\text{reference state}).
\]

Perceptual systems also integrate change over characteristic windows. Different organisms can therefore resolve the same external dynamics at different temporal grain.

This does not imply that physical time itself is discrete.

It does show that:

> **experienced smoothness is partly a property of the relation between process scale and observer scale.**

ARA adds the possibility that a temporal sequence which appears line-like or continuous at one rung may be a local cut through a larger closure.

Again, this is a hypothesis about the geometry of time, not an established result.

# 13. Why not the ellipse, polygon, spiral, or line?

The circle is not the only closed or recurring shape, so it is worth asking why ARA gives it a privileged role.

| Geometry | What it provides | Why it is not the same primitive |
|---|---|---|
| **Circle** | Smooth finite closure, constant curvature, no vertices, scale similarity, continuous traversal | Natural candidate for isotropic pure closure |
| **Ellipse** | Smooth finite closure | Has preferred axes and varying curvature; useful as a distorted or projected circle |
| **Polygon** | Finite closure with explicit relations and junctions | Privileges vertices and edges; useful as a discrete relational scaffold |
| **Line** | Locally simple traversal | No finite Euclidean closure; can be a local or infinite-radius limit of circular curvature |
| **Spiral** | Continuous movement and scale change | Does not locally return to the same closure; useful for handover/nonclosure rather than pure local identity |
| **Torus** | Multiple periodic directions and closed circulation | Already a compound topology with more than one cycle; not minimal planar closure |
| **Sphere** | Isotropic 3-D closure | Not a competitor; it is the directionally decompressed version of the circle in ARA |

The ellipse is particularly important.

ARA does not need to deny ellipses, asymmetries, dents, lobes, or more complex observed shapes.

Instead it proposes:

\[
\text{observed identity}
=
\text{pure closure}
+
\text{projection}
+
\text{couplings}
+
\text{history}.
\]

The distortion is not discarded.

It is information.

If the undistorted identity is spherical, then the difference between that pure geometry and the observed geometry can encode the relations that are currently moulding it.

That statement is ARA interpretation, not a theorem of Euclidean geometry, but the circle supplies an unusually clean geometric model for it.

# 21. What the circle does not prove

The circle is an unusually good geometric candidate for ARA, but several conclusions do not follow from geometry alone.

This paper does **not** establish that:

- physical reality is infinitely recursive;
- the Planck scale is the bottom ARA rung;
- there is any bottom rung at all;
- every physical line eventually closes into a larger circle;
- every pure physical identity is literally Euclidean;
- the octave factor \(2\) is forced by circular geometry;
- Phi is a universal time constant;
- physical time is discrete;
- subjective experience is identical after rung normalisation;
- all observed distortions can be uniquely inverted into a pure sphere;
- or a sphere ontology is more than a powerful coordinate language.

Those are separate hypotheses.

The value of “why the circle?” is narrower and stronger:

> **If ARA requires a geometry that supports continuous closure, uninterrupted movement, no privileged boundary point, similarity across scale, local line-like behaviour, arbitrary orientation cuts, and recursive embedding, the circle/sphere is an exceptionally natural candidate.**

The empirical question is whether reality needs that candidate, or merely permits it.

# 22. A compact evidence ladder

The argument can be separated into four levels.

### Level 1 — Exact established geometry

- A circle has constant curvature.
- A circle has no vertices.
- A regular \(n\)-gon distributes total turning \(2\pi\) across \(n\) vertices.
- Large-radius circular arcs become locally line-like.

# 25. Canonical synthesis

> **Why the circle?**  
> ARA proposes the circle as the planar expression of continuous relational closure. A finite polygon localises relation into edges and directional handover into vertices; in the circle, the same total turning is distributed continuously, leaving no privileged junction. Movement can continue indefinitely while the identity remains closed. Because circular geometry is similarity-invariant, changing scale changes native magnitude without changing relational form. Because sufficiently shallow curvature appears locally straight, a child can observe only a line-like segment of a much larger closure. Because projection changes appearance without necessarily changing identity, a circle or sphere can present differently under different cuts and coupling channels. Decompressed across orientation, the circle becomes the sphere; a \(0\!-\!2\) ARA value is one oriented diameter through that identity rather than the identity itself.
>
> ARA then adds the stronger hypothesis that physical identities actually instantiate this geometry recursively: smaller complete closures remain embedded as relational structure inside larger closures; local closure is carried by parent movement; and apparent lines, discontinuities, distortions, or absences may sometimes reflect scale, orientation, projection, or weak coupling rather than fundamentally different underlying forms.
>
> The circle does not prove the framework. It is the geometry that makes the framework's central claim coherent: **identity can remain itself while continuously moving, changing scale, changing appearance, and becoming a child inside a larger identity.**
