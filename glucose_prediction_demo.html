Can we see glucose 15 minutes ahead?

A small experiment on real (anonymised) type-1 CGM data — what the recent trend can and can’t tell you.

The last few readings have momentum

Glucose drifts rather than jumping at random, so from just the recent trace the next ~15–30 minutes are fairly predictable. Grey line = the real glucose (this stretch swings from a low through to a high). Blue dots = where a simple model, given no meal or insulin information at all, predicted it would be 15 minutes earlier.

Glucose with 15-minute-ahead predictions.

Shaded band = a common in-range zone (3.9–10 mmol/L). Values in mmol/L (×18 ≈ mg/dL).

Why it gives up at an hour

Past about 30 minutes the trend alone stops helping — and that’s the interesting bit. By an hour out, glucose isn’t really being steered by its recent drift anymore. It’s being steered by what you ate and the insulin on board. The model can’t see those, so it can’t see past the wall.

So the maths quietly points back at the thing every person with diabetes already knows runs the show: food and insulin. The next version would feed those in to reach further ahead.

Please read this part. This is a curiosity-driven research demo on a handful of anonymised recordings — not a medical device, not advice, and not anything to base dosing or treatment on. The short-horizon idea here is already built, properly and safely, into real insulin pumps (predictive low-glucose suspend). Always go by your own CGM, your pump, and your care team.
